Arithmetic Reasoning

Word problems, rates, ratios, percentages and units — explanations and worked examples to complement your practice questions.

Turn language into equations

Arithmetic Reasoning tests how well you translate an everyday situation into a calculation. First identify what the problem asks you to find, then list the quantities and their units. A useful habit is to estimate the answer before calculating: if you buy eight items for $64, each item should cost around $8, not $80.

Worked example: A warehouse receives 18 boxes with 24 batteries in each box. It ships 150 batteries. How many remain?

Compute the starting quantity: 18 × 24 = 432. Subtract shipped units: 432 − 150 = 282 batteries.

Percentages and discounts

Percent means “per hundred.” Convert 15% to 0.15 before multiplying. A 15% discount on a $72 item is $72 × 0.15 = $10.80, so the sale price is $61.20.

For a percentage increase, multiply by 1 plus the decimal rate. Increasing $80 by 12% gives $80 × 1.12 = $89.60. Don't confuse the change in price with the final price.

Ratios, proportions and unit rates

Write ratios with consistent units and compare like to like. A machine produces 75 components in 5 minutes. Its unit rate is 75 ÷ 5 = 15 components per minute. In 12 minutes at the same rate, it can produce 180 components.

When the rate is expressed per hour but the time is in minutes, convert first: 30 minutes = 0.5 hour. At 46 miles per hour, distance is 46 × 0.5 = 23 miles.

How to practice

For each word problem, underline the requested quantity, convert units, write a calculation, and check whether the answer is reasonable. Watch for “total,” “remaining,” “per,” “twice,” and “of,” which often indicate the necessary operation. Practice without a calculator to develop efficient mental arithmetic and estimation.
